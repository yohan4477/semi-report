[Investment in Korea 101 – Ep. 3: Who You're Really Buying From] 

Foreign buyers evaluating a Korean mid-cap tend to spend most of their diligence on the asset, the numbers, the customer base, the competitive position. Far less goes toward the seller. That is usually the more expensive oversight, because in Korea's mid-market, who is selling shapes the negotiation as much as what is being sold. Sellers here fall into three types, and each plays by different rules.

The most common is the owner-driven seller, the founder or founding family, and it shows up in two moods, both traceable to the inheritance tax pressure covered in Episode 2. 

In the first, the founder is still alive and still in control, selling on his own terms before the tax clock forces someone else's hand. He will frame the sale around legacy, continuity for employees, a buyer who treats the business the way he did. Take that at face value and you'll misplay the negotiation. In my experience, legacy is less a real condition than a story the founder needs to justify the sale to himself. The number still has to clear his target. What changes is the packaging — wrap it in credible commitments on employment and continuity, and the deal moves. Deliver a bare wire transfer, and you can lose a deal you technically outbid for.

The second mood belongs to the heir who has already inherited the shares. The tax clock isn't a future risk here — it's running now. An installment plan may extend payment over several years, but most heirs need liquidity sooner, and have far less appetite than their parents did for running an industrial business. Unlike the founder, time is not on this seller's side. That's real leverage, for a buyer who recognizes it.

A second category has nothing to do with family: the conglomerate divesting a non-core subsidiary as part of a group reshuffle. These carve-outs run bigger and messier than they look — tangled in shared services and intercompany dependencies that need a proper transition agreement to unwind — and they carry the highest labor exposure of the three, since union consent is often a precondition to closing at all.

The third is the domestic private equity fund exiting at the end of a holding period. No legacy, no reluctance — just a return target, a deadline, and a process. These sales run as auctions because they are auctions, and they reward speed and credibility over sentiment.

No single playbook works across all three. A founder needs to believe you'll honor what he built — even as the number does the real work. A corporate parent needs to believe you can execute cleanly and absorb the labor question. A PE seller just needs you to close on time. 

Same target, same valuation model, three different paths to winning. Knowing who's actually across the table is the first piece of diligence that matters.
