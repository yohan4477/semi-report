BTS is back — all seven members — after nearly four years. Walking through Gwanghwamun on my way home tonight, the energy was unmistakable. Brands had activated everywhere, and the foreign fans who flew in specifically for this weren't just a trickle. They were everywhere.

The word hallyu (Korean Wave) has been around for decades, but if you had to pick the moment it truly went global, most would point to Psy's "Gangnam Style" in 2012. What's happened since is something different, though. Korean culture hasn't just gone viral — it's been quietly, steadily moving into the mainstream. Music, film, food, beauty. The trajectory is consistent.

For anyone watching Korea from a business or investment perspective, tonight's event is worth paying attention to beyond the spectacle.

This is a country where a single pop group's comeback required the President to personally address airport congestion, deployed 6,700 police officers, and shut down central Seoul for 33 hours. The economic footprint — estimated at around ₩270 billion by Bloomberg — flows through hotels, retail, content licensing, and inbound tourism simultaneously.

There's a concept in Korean financial markets called the Korea Discount — the persistent undervaluation of Korean equities relative to global peers, often attributed to governance issues and geopolitical risk. But what's building on the cultural side tells a different story. Soft power is accumulating. Brand equity is compounding. Consumer trust in Korean products and content is growing across demographics and geographies.

The discount may still exist on a Bloomberg terminal. On the ground in Gwanghwamun tonight, it was hard to see it.

한류라는 단어가 등장한 지는 꽤 됐지만, 전 세계의 시선을 한 번에 끌어모은 건 아마 싸이의 '강남스타일'이었을 겁니다. 그리고 그 이후로 한국 문화는 조용히, 그러나 꾸준히 글로벌 메인스트림 안으로 파고들었습니다.
