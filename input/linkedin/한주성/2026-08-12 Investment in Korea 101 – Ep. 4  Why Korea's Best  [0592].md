[Investment in Korea 101 – Ep. 4: Why Korea's Best Deals May Never Reach an Auction]

In Episode 3, I wrote about the three types of sellers foreign buyers are most likely to encounter in Korea: founders, corporate groups, and private equity funds.

But before negotiating with any of them, you first have to find the deal.

Foreign investors entering Korea often start in the obvious place. They build relationships with investment banks, receive teasers, review auction processes, and wait for attractive businesses to come to market.

There is nothing wrong with that. Many good Korean companies are sold through competitive processes, particularly larger assets, corporate carve-outs and PE exits.

But if that is your only source of deals, you may be seeing only part of the market.

The reason goes back to Episodes 1 and 2. Many Korean mid-sized businesses were never built to be sold. Their founders may have spent thirty or forty years building the company without ever considering an exit. When succession finally becomes a problem, their first call is not necessarily to an investment bank.

It may be to an accountant who has advised the family for decades. A lawyer they trust. A business partner. Or someone in the same industry.

For many first-generation founders, selling the company is not simply a financial transaction. It is one of the most personal decisions they will make.

Before asking, "How much can I get?", the first question may be:

"Who can I trust with the company?"

This creates a part of Korea's mid-market that can be difficult to see from outside. Some opportunities develop quietly through relationships long before an information memorandum is written. Others may never become broad auctions at all.

For foreign buyers, the implication is important: deal sourcing in Korea cannot be entirely reactive.

Waiting for a polished teaser means entering after the owner has decided to sell, hired an adviser and potentially invited several competing buyers.

The more interesting opportunity may be one step earlier: identifying businesses where succession, ownership transition or strategic change could create a transaction before a formal process begins.

That does not mean sending acquisition proposals to hundreds of founders. A generic approach can close a door rather than open one.

It means understanding the ownership, the founder's situation and whether there is a genuine reason for the owner to consider selling.

This is why local networks matter in Korean M&A — not because deals happen in some mysterious back room, but because the decision to sell often begins as a relationship before it becomes a transaction.

The auction market shows you companies that are already for sale.

The harder part, and sometimes the more interesting part, is finding the ones that are about to be.
