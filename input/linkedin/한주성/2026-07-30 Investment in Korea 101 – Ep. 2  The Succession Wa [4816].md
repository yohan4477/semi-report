[Investment in Korea 101 – Ep. 2: The Succession Wave]

In Episode 1, I made the case that Korea's best mid-market companies were never really for sale — not because of foreign ownership restrictions, but because their founders never built them to be sold. That's changing now. Here's why.

Start with demographics. The generation that built much of Korea's industrial base — founders who launched manufacturing and industrial-services businesses in the 1970s and 80s — is now in its seventies and eighties. Many built genuinely excellent companies. Fewer built a plan for what comes next. Children have gone into other careers, moved abroad, or simply have no interest in running a factory.

Layered on top is Korea's inheritance tax, and this is where most foreign investors underestimate the arithmetic. Korea's inheritance tax reaches up to 50 percent, rising to 60 percent when business shares transfer along with management control — among the highest combinations in the OECD, where fourteen of thirty-eight member countries impose no inheritance tax at all. When Samsung's founding family settled the tax on the late chairman's estate, the bill came to roughly $8 billion — the largest such payment in Korean history. That's the extreme end. 

For an unlisted mid-cap worth a fraction of that, the same rate structure can still leave an heir facing a bill larger than they can pay without selling the company itself.

For a lot of founders, that changes what "handing it down" means. Passing the business to your children doesn't preserve it — it can hand them a bill large enough to force a sale on someone else's terms, at a time they didn't choose. Increasingly, founders are getting ahead of that: selling deliberately, while they still control price, buyer, and timing.

That shift — from "never for sale" to "better to sell now, on my terms" — is what's driving a wave of quality mid-market Korean businesses toward the market for the first time in a generation.

One caveat: this pressure may not be permanent. There's an active push to cut the top rate, with reform proposals ranging toward 30 percent, and the system itself may be restructured in the coming years. If reform lands, this window could narrow. For now, the law hasn't changed — and founders reaching retirement today are deciding under the regime that exists now.

For foreign buyers, this isn't a demographic footnote. It means the next several years are likely to bring more genuinely motivated mid-market sellers than Korea has produced in decades.
