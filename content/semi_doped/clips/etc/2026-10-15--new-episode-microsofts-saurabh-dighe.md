## Maia 300 Architecture: Data Flow and Low Precision

**Yeah, that’s good. And feel free as we talk through these architectural decisions, like you mentioned, the workload now demands with the agentic, like a long-running kind of persistent KV cache, lots of memory bandwidth, memory capacity, but continue to sprinkle in your thoughts on how the workloads are shaping your design decisions because when we see that Pareto frontier with throughput on the Y-axis and interactivity on the X-axis, I’m always interested in what the actual deployment and the companies are choosing. Obviously they’re not necessarily filling out that whole curve, so I’m always curious what is the class of workload, like you said, that would land at a certain point? Is it higher throughput or lower latency? So again, as we’ll go through this here, but definitely feel free to sprinkle that in because I don’t think we get a lot of detail about exactly which points people are sort of optimizing for.**

**Saurabh** : We’ll do. One of the things that we believe in as we look at the first principles of how you’re designing an AI inference system—AI inferencing to me is not a compute problem only. It’s actually a data movement problem. And so we decided that instead of trying to attack the compute problem, let’s see how we can create a new class of architecture that really focuses on the data movement energy. The industry has done an excellent job over the last few years with new microarchitecture, new process technology, and new… but what has not kept up is reducing data movement, because that is a significant factor—sometimes two orders of magnitude in terms of the energy you could consume to move data and keep the compute engine fed.

One of the fundamental principles of our architecture is what we call the software-defined data flow architecture, which we introduced in Maia 200 and continue to enhance. The underlying thesis is that with AI inferencing, we can actually plan for the data movement ahead of time. Because we can plan for the data movement ahead of time and we know what these data movement patterns look like, we can have distributed memories and pin the data at the right location for the right time. We can hide communication and computation and continue to improve the efficiency of data movement in this architecture.

Now with AI-assisted kernel development, it has become even more efficient. We don’t need armies of software people to program this system; we can have agents optimize kernels. To your point, if the workload is LLM inferencing, at the heart of it, we believe the data flow architecture gives the best energy efficiency for inferencing per token. So that’s where we started.

At the same time, we said, okay, that’s great, but we need to be able to optimize the compute engine and the data engines together in one system. We then took an approach where we had distributed memories on the die, so we use a lot of SRAM. We use it as a scratchpad, and we expose the programmability and the explicit data movement instructions back into the compiler of the software layer. We’re also able to create much better computation and communication patterns on-die. We have the SRAM with our tensor unit and our vector unit, but we also have a global NoC that distributes the data within the chips inside the package. That really helps us again reduce the data movement.

We’ve gone towards a more NUMA-based architecture, which means that once you’ve placed the data close to your compute, you don’t have to drag it from another memory controller or PHY sitting on the other side of the chip. A lot of thought has gone into how to keep the data movement, data storage, and data computation at the lowest energy possible.

As we move outside the SoC, we look at how to connect these chips together. A lot of times people think of this as just putting a network to connect these chips together, but we thought of the network as an extension of the data flow architecture. What that means is that specialized data movement engines, or DMAs, and the communication protocol that goes on top can allow chips in other nodes, servers, and racks to talk through the same communication paradigm as what they’re doing on the chip as well. It’s just an extension of that data flow architecture.

Finally, we also made sure that we drive a lot of rack-scale efficiency. Because in the end, what matters is the deployment. People don’t care about just the chip; they care about how the entire deployment delivers more tokens at a lower operating cost. That’s where our rack-scale architecture really helps us build a more efficient deployment scenario in our data centers. So, there are lots of layers of optimization, right from the silicon itself all the way to how we deploy and allocate power in the data centers.

## Maia 300 System Specs: Racks, HBM4, and Chiplets

**Okay, so the framing with the Maia 300 and agentic AI is all about minimizing data movement. You’re co-designing all the way down from the amount of SRAM on the chip to the local HBM, to the software for developers to manage this, all the way to the networking and how you’re communicating across chips because, of course, on a scale-up fabric they’re sharing memory, so to speak, with DMA. All the way to rack-scale, how many accelerators can we fit into this rack, and so on.**

**For listeners who haven’t read the recent announcements, can you just zoom out and give us the high level of what the Maia 300 looks like? How many chips are in a rack, and so on? Then we’ll dive into the memory specifically. I want to dig into the SRAM versus HBM tradeoffs and decisions you made and also maybe comparing against Maia 200, like HBM3E to HBM4. But yeah, maybe just zoom us back out and remind us at a high level of some of the specs for the Maia 300.**

**Saurabh** : Absolutely. What we’ve announced today is really our conviction of the Maia inference system architecture. We have Maia 200 in production, outperforming some of the GPUs that are in our fleet today. With that evidence and production-proven success, we are saying that we’re going to be scaling Maia to multiple gigawatt deployments starting in 2027. That’s a pretty significant strategic positioning for us as Microsoft, and it’ll be a foundational element of all Microsoft products and services in the future.

Now, Maia 300 specifically, what we’re saying is that over Maia 200, we are expecting this rack-scale architecture to deliver 7x more token throughput per rack than what we could do with Maia 200. And it will be delivered at 2.5x more performance per watt.

So, the interesting story now is how do we get there? That’s what we’re excited to talk about. As you look at it, we talked a little bit about the software-defined data flow architecture—that’s really a foundational element. We also went to multiple chiplets. We went to a TSMC N2P process, and with a chiplet architecture, we’re really able to balance how much flops we want, how much HBM we want in the system, and how much IO or scale-up bandwidth we want in the system. So we’ve tuned the system a lot more to delivering, again, not stranding flops, not stranding resources in the system, but delivering that useful intelligence through every watt. A multi-chiplet architecture really helped us there.

In addition, we’ve introduced much better arithmetic, so low-precision arithmetic. We can talk about how with agentic use cases and with more persistent and growing KV, these MXFP4-like formats really help us optimize our memory. With lower-precision data formats, we have optimized model weight storage in four-bit or lower precision. At the same time, we can optimize the KV with some of these lower precisions. On the compute side, we can have computation that’s FP4 by FP8 so that we can continue to keep the accuracy and the quality of the model output, while at the same time packing more and more concurrency and higher KV in the same HBM. So, a lot of thought went into how we use lower-precision math.

A lot of thought went into how we actually design the memory subsystem. Like we said, we have the on-die SRAM, but at the same time, we’ve also gone to HBM4. We have got eight stacks of HBM4 on Maia 300, delivering a total of 18 terabytes per second of HBM bandwidth. That’s almost 2.5x of what we did on Maia 200. And if you look at the rack-scale level, that’s 5x of what we could do on Maia 200. That really was a focus for us again, going back to how do we manage these longer contexts, more persistent KVs, and decode-bound or memory-bound, decode-heavy workloads.

Our scale-up network is the same scale-up network that we use from a server all the way to a superpod, which is now 12,288 accelerators in a superpod configuration, or 96 accelerators in a pod, which we call a rack. In a rack, we will now have 96 accelerators, which is double what we did on Maia 200. And even though we doubled the number of accelerators, we can deliver this at 167 kilowatts per rack, which is extremely energy efficient if you compare it to anything else that you’d be getting from other vendors next year. So, 96 accelerators at 167 kilowatts and then being able to scale up with one scale-up fabric from rack to superpod to about more than 12,000 accelerators is really how we’ve spent our time creating a really unique scale-up fabric.

**Fascinating. Let’s get into the scale-up fabric here in a second because it’s very interesting to go so hard into the scale-up fabric and make that a big bet there. So I want to unpack that. But first, the HBM. You went from six HBM3E stacks to eight HBM4 stacks and the bandwidth went up, I think you said maybe two, two and a half times, and capacity went up per accelerator. So zooming out, the amount of bandwidth and capacity went up more than the compute. So maybe again, it sounds like you were really making decisions here around the decode side of things and the memory side of things for KV cache. How do you decide—obviously if you add more bandwidth and capacity, you can actually unlock more of the flops if you’re not keeping the compute waiting on the memory. How do you thread that needle and decide how much more of your die area you want to commit to flops versus HBM? And also, relatedly, remind me, do you guys have just one compute die with the eight HBM stacks around it? And then maybe contrast with others who are doing two compute dies.**

**Saurabh** : This is a great question and it really comes down to what problem are we trying to solve and why. Like I said, we continue to focus on inferencing as our primary. For inference-based workloads, we wanted to make sure that we had a balanced system where we are able to expand our memory bandwidth and capacity, like we talked about, while keeping flops continuing to increase. We did move to N2P because we believe that that’s the most efficient process node for computation flops. We went to a full-reticle size compute, but at the same time, we felt that going towards two compute dies would have not been the right architectural choice for the workloads and the shape of the workloads that we are seeing.

Instead, what we did was we still wanted the eight HBM stacks on HBM4. This is where our chiplet architecture really came into play, where we were able to create these memory interface dies to extend the shoreline on either side of the die. We’re able to extend the shoreline with a chiplet architecture, put four HBMs on either side of that, while still keeping it as one very efficient compute die.

Now going from HBM3E to HBM4, you get those two multiplier effects. On one end, picojoule per bit is super critical for HBM data transfer. With HBM3 to HBM4, the industry has moved to a wider interface but kept the pin speeds relatively the same. What happens is you end up with a much better data transfer rate and energy profile for the data transfer rate. That’s one multiplier you get. And then obviously the second multiplier is when you go from six stacks of HBM3E on Maia 200 to eight stacks of HBM4. So if you put those two things together along with our multi-chiplet architecture, you go from something that’s delivering 7 terabytes per second with a certain amount of flops to now two and a half times that—18 terabytes per second—and with another around 35% to 37% of flop increase from something like 750 watts to 950 watts. With just a 200-watt increase, we were extremely cognizant of the fact that when we put power into these SoCs and into our systems, it needs to translate to significantly better intelligence outcome. We did not want to strand any of those resources. So those are the thoughts that went behind balancing the machine towards these workloads.

**That’s an interesting figure. Got it, got it. Yes, increasing your bandwidth per watt is a very interesting figure of merit and it makes a lot of sense and it aligns with your earlier framing of thinking a lot about data movement. Okay, so you’re talking about a lot of decisions that are very beneficial for decode, for the memory-bound part of the workload. Maybe talk to me a little bit about pre-fill. I mean, within a rack of 96 accelerators, will you still ultimately disaggregate pre-fill and decode and what does that look like?**

**Saurabh** : Just a quick correction. We go from HBM3E to HBM4, not 4E. But everything else you said, absolutely correct. It’s really two things, right? One is we are trying to optimize both for energy efficiency and cost. When you look at energy efficiency, you get a much better picojoule per bit, or overall transfer bandwidth per watt is a better profile when we go to the wider interface; you get a lot more bits coming out. At the same time, we also wanted to make sure that we are using the right process technology for the right function. For compute, you want to get to N2P. For some of the HBM and HBM PHYs, we decided that we’ll have a different process technology to be able to manage the cost and the power profile of going to HBM4 as well. That really drives our philosophy on how we make sure that we are more cost-efficient over a generation and more energy-efficient per generation. The decisions to go chiplet really helped us there.

Absolutely. We did a lot of modeling on understanding what our pre-fill to decode ratios are inside the fleet. We’re pretty confident that even with what we have in our chips and over the rack, we can absolutely deliver the pre-fill to decode ratios. You bring up a good point: what is the pre-fill decode disaggregation strategy? This is where our strength in networking comes along because we have one scale-up fabric. We can absolutely take instances of pre-fill that have multiple GPUs and position them in one rack or spread them across racks, and then instances of decode in the same scale-up fabric. This really helps us because we don’t have to go over a scale-out network to pass the KV from a pre-fill to a decode position. We can do that all over the same scale-up. We do run our fleet with disaggregation and we are able to take some of those Maias and run them as a pre-fill instance and some of them as decode. When we match-rate them, depending on how many pre-fill machines you have and what is the token output on those machines, we try to match that with our decode side as well.

## Scale-Up Fabric and Network Topology

**Nice, got it. Okay, so let’s go into networking topology. I know yours is two-tiered, but tell us, zooming out, people like to think about where is it copper, where is it optics? How fast are the links? How many accelerators does it connect? And maybe even at a high level, if you can fit 96 accelerators in one rack and you’re doing mixture-of-experts inference at scale, why do you even need to go beyond one rack? Why need superpods?**

**Saurabh** : It’s a good question. Let me build it up from the bottom. At the lowest level of our network topology, we have our integrated NICs. We introduced them in Maia 200 and we continue to advance the capability of those NICs. They are on-die NICs, extremely area and power efficient, and they’re really built for scale-up. We’re not trying to solve for millions of endpoints talking or a general cloud network NIC kind of thing. We’re basically looking at what a scale-up domain looks like. It needs to be extremely high performance, it needs to be low latency, and we are going to continue to increase the bandwidth. That’s what a tensor parallelism or some form of workload that’s spread across nodes in the rack would need. Looking at the communication patterns, we’ve optimized the NIC.

From there, we have used standard Ethernet switches and cables because we do believe, again with the philosophy of maintaining the cost and not trying to go into more exotic implementations, we’ve stayed on Ethernet to really benefit from the scale economics of that industry. We have built a node topology that keeps some of those links within the server itself because we believe that neighboring Maias would have much higher communication, especially if you’re doing tensor parallelism. So we’re taking the tensors and spreading them across a couple of Maias in the node. A lot of the bandwidth stays within the node and then half of that bandwidth goes to the switch links. That’s the first tier of switch, and all 96 accelerators have connections to the first tier of switch.

Then we also decided that as models grew bigger or as we started to look at higher sharding schemes, or we thought of flexible job placement so that the software allocator’s orchestration becomes a lot easier, we felt that a second tier of network would really help us scale into those use cases. We have our own transport on top of Ethernet, so we were able to then use some of those transport features to be able to say, yes, I can actually scale now and have a second tier. We can have accelerators from one rack talk to another accelerator from another rack using the same communication stack that’s been developed for in-rack as well as cross-rack. That really helps us now get into this place where you can start expanding the cluster size to about, let’s say, 128 racks in the same scale-up domain.

Copper has a reach, so we stayed with copper inside the rack. But as soon as we went from the switch in the rack to the next level of switch, we started using optics. You’ll start seeing some of those systems come next year as well, where you’ll start seeing more and more optics being used in the scale-up domain, really to find that reach and to build these larger and larger pods or superpods.

**Got it. Okay. This is really interesting. So you said at first, you don’t really need to design around scale-out because you’re not trying to do a million accelerators for a training job; you’re just focused on inference as we talked about before. So you’re really focused on scale-up. You said there are four accelerators that are tightly coupled to each other, directly connected, and you’re thinking about splitting up the workload so that neighbors talk to each other a lot directly over copper. All the accelerators in the rack are connected via the first level of switch to each other, also copper. And then the switches connect to other switches, rack to rack, using optics. Awesome, interesting. And it sounds very powerful and flexible. Do you have any two cents or are you guys saying anything about how Microsoft feels on near-package optics versus CPO, NPO versus CPO?**

**Saurabh** : The story is developing. We do believe that optics in scale-up is something that everybody will eventually move towards. At the same time, I think we need to bring the reliability and the cost of optics along with the right supply chain motion to make sure that the industry can all move together in some ways. In terms of NPO and CPO, there are real trade-offs there. Talking about what we do with reliability, with NPO, you have a less disruptive, much more streamlined path into the server and how you design it. But over time, we also know that CPO gives you the power efficiency at extremely high bandwidths. I think the industry is going to start with a little bit of NPO, trying to get a feel of how we manufacture this, how we make this very serviceable, how we improve the reliability and the cost, and then move towards a more integrated approach, which is CPO on the XPU side. Similarly on the switch side, we’re already starting to see some of those motions as well.

## Software, Deployment, and The Big Picture

**That makes sense. Okay, so when you’re talking about your internal customers, how much are they using Maia 200 today? And then as far as Maia 300 goes, from a software perspective, you talked about the software-defined data flow architecture. Is it going to be a big lift for those internal customers to move to the next version of Maia?**
